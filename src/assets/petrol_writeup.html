This dataset is a snapshot of petrol markets across 181 countries and territories, dated June 23, 2022, a moment when fuel prices were already under heavy global pressure. Each row describes one economy through nine measurements: how much oil it burns each day, its share of world consumption, its yearly gallons per person, several expressions of the pump price, its income per person, and two derived affordability figures. The table is ordered by total consumption, so the story begins at the top with the two heavyweights. The United States consumes 19,687,287 barrels of oil per day, which is 20% of the world total. China follows with 12,791,553 barrels and a 13% share. Taken together, the first two rows account for roughly a third of all the oil consumed by the countries in the table, a concentration that sets the tone for everything else.

Consumption falls away quickly after those two. India is a distant third at 4,443,000 barrels per day, and Japan is close behind with 4,012,877. Russia records 3,631,287, while Saudi Arabia, one of the world's great producers, still burns 3,302,000 barrels at home. By my calculation the top ten rows make up about 60% of the total daily consumption in the entire dataset. The bottom of the table is a different universe. Niue consumes just 51 barrels a day, and Saint Helena uses 70. Because the shares are rounded to whole percentages, the world share column loses nearly all its resolution in the tail: 147 of the 181 rows show 0%, including the last row, so small consumers are effectively indistinguishable in that column and are better compared using the raw barrel counts.

Total consumption mostly reflects population, so the per-person figures in the yearly gallons column tell a more interesting story. The United States uses 934.3 gallons per person each year, but China uses only 138.7 and India just 51.4, which shows that the two largest consumers by volume are not the heaviest users per head. The top of that ranking belongs to Singapore, at an extraordinary 3,679.5 gallons, more than double the next-highest entries: the tiny territory Saint Pierre & Miquelon at 1,705.1, Malta at 1,652.2, and Saudi Arabia at 1,560.2. Such figures are likely inflated by shipping, refining, aviation bunkering, and other activity that is not personal driving, so they should be read as national intensity rather than what an individual buys at the pump. At the opposite extreme, Burundi registers only 2.2 gallons per person, a gap of more than a thousandfold from Singapore.

Pump prices are recorded three ways: in dollars per gallon, dollars per liter, and Pakistani rupees per liter. The middle of the distribution sits near $5 per gallon; by my calculation the median is $5.28 and the mean about $5.70, with a long tail of expensive outliers pulling the average upward. The United States is close to the middle at $5.19, and India is slightly lower at $5.05. Europe is markedly more expensive. Germany charges $7.65 per gallon, France $8.27, and the United Kingdom $8.38, while the Netherlands reaches $9.33. Nordic countries push higher still, with Norway at $10.22 and Denmark at $10.04. The most expensive large economy in this group is Hong Kong, at $11.35. The same prices can be restated per liter: Germany's gallon price converts to $2.02 per liter, and in rupee terms to 427.44 PKR, which suggests the dataset was built with a Pakistani audience in mind.

That perspective makes the Pakistan row a useful benchmark. Pakistan pays $3.90 per gallon, or $1.03 per liter and 217.85 PKR, well below Western Europe but far above the cheapest countries. Those cheapest countries are the most striking feature of the price columns. Venezuela sells petrol for just $0.08 per gallon, or 4.65 PKR per liter. Libya is nearly as cheap at $0.12, followed by Iran at $0.20, which is only 11.21 PKR per liter, and Brunei at $0.83. Only these four rows fall under one dollar per gallon. All are oil producers with heavily subsidized domestic fuel, a pattern that continues just above the threshold with Kuwait at $1.29 and Nigeria at $1.57. Cheap fuel does not always mean heavy use, however: Nigeria's 35.3 gallons per person is low, while Iran's 347.6 is close to Russia's 383.2 despite a fraction of the price.

At the other end, one row stands so far outside the pattern that it deserves scrutiny. North Korea is listed at $54.89 per gallon, $14.50 per liter, and 3,066.75 PKR, more than three times the next-highest price. The country has very low consumption (18,000 barrels per day and 10.9 gallons per person) and a tiny income of $1,300, so this figure probably reflects a scarce, tightly controlled market rather than a price ordinary residents pay. Even ignoring it, the expensive tail includes small islands: Tonga at $16.20 and Niue at $11.43, both probably driven by the cost of shipping fuel to remote locations. Equally notable is the Central African Republic, where petrol costs $9.06 per gallon in a country with an income of only $477 per person, an outcome of landlocked logistics and limited supply.

Income is where prices turn into hardship. The GDP per capita column ranges from $115,874 in Luxembourg and $87,097 in Switzerland down to just $274 in Burundi. The dataset converts this into two affordability measures, the last two columns, which show how many gallons a year's average income would buy and how many multiples of the country's yearly per-person consumption that represents. For the United States, income buys 12,218 gallons, or 13 times the average annual consumption. The champion is Venezuela: its income buys 200,700 gallons, or 654 times its yearly usage, because of that near-zero price. Brunei (33,064), Libya (30,825), and Qatar (22,993) also stand out. The weakest positions belong to North Korea at 24 gallons, the Central African Republic at 53, and Burundi at 54, where a full year of average income buys only a few dozen gallons.

The final affordability column is subtler and needs careful reading, since it depends on both income and habits. Switzerland scores 25 despite a high price of $8.27, because its wealth is enormous relative to its consumption, whereas Luxembourg, with the highest income, scores only 10 because its 1,487.2 gallons per person are so high. The lowest scores occur where consumption is high relative to income: Singapore and Lebanon both show 2 and 2, and Seychelles hits the floor at 1. Two caveats apply. First, GDP per capita is an average and hides inequality, so real hardship is greater than the figures suggest for poorer households. Second, income and consumption are only loosely tied to price; by my calculation, GDP per capita correlates fairly strongly with gallons per person (about 0.62) but only weakly with price per gallon (about 0.18). In other words, the wealthy burn more fuel but do not necessarily pay more for it. Taken as a whole, the table shows that petrol is a story of extremes: consumption concentrated in a handful of giants, prices set as much by subsidy and geography as by markets, and affordability that varies by a factor of several thousand between the richest and poorest rows.
